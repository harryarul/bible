Song of Solomon

Shir ha-Shirim · the Song of Songs

“Set me as a seal upon thine heart… for love is strong as death.”

Song of Solomon 8:6 · KJV

Eight chapters. One song. The title means the Song of Songs — the finest of all, the superlative of love poems. It is a sustained lyric passed back and forth between a bride, her beloved, and the watching daughters of Jerusalem, set in vineyards, gardens, and the streets of the city at night.

For centuries it has been read on two levels at once: a canonical celebration of covenant love between husband and wife, and a picture of the love between God and his people — Christ and the Church. This study walks chapter by chapter, marking the turning points and the verses worth carrying with you.

The Voices

Three speakers carry the song, often without introduction — you tell them apart by their words.

The Bride — the Shulamite, sun-darkened, seeking.
The Beloved — the bridegroom, the king.
The Daughters — the chorus of Jerusalem.

The Shape

Not one straight narrative but a weave of songs: longing, praise, a night search, a wedding procession, separation and reunion, and a final seal.

8 chapters · 117 verses · traditionally ascribed to Solomon (1:1).

The Refrain

One line keeps returning, in growing fullness — the heartbeat of the whole book.

“My beloved is mine, and I am his.”
2:16 → 6:3 → 7:10

Chapter by Chapter

Walking the Song

I
Chapter One

Longing & First Praise

“Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth: for thy love is better than wine.” Song 1:2

The song opens mid-desire. The bride calls for her beloved, owns that she is “black, but comely” — darkened from working her brothers’ vineyards — and asks where he pastures his flock. He answers with praise, she with adoration. Their love is already a place of belonging.

  • “Draw me, we will run after thee” — desire that pulls others along 1:4
  • She is compared to “a company of horses in Pharaoh’s chariots” — royal, striking 1:9
  • A green bed, cedar beams — their meeting place is a living garden 1:16–17
desirebelongingthe pursuit
Reflect
  1. “Draw me, we will run after thee.” Where does your own being drawn toward God spill over and pull others along?
  2. She admits her own vineyard she has “not kept.” What have you neglected while tending everyone else’s expectations?

Reading frameLove begins as longing before it becomes anything else.

II
Chapter Two

Rose of Sharon, Voice of the Beloved

“I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys.” Song 2:1

The most quoted chapter. The beloved is an apple tree among the trees of the wood; his banner over her is love. Then his voice arrives over the mountains with a springtime invitation — the winter is past, the flowers appear, rise up and come away.

  • “His banner over me was love” — love as open, flown declaration 2:4
  • “Rise up, my love… the winter is past” — the call into a new season 2:10–11
  • “Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines” — guard love from small ruins 2:15
  • First refrain: “My beloved is mine, and I am his” 2:16
springtimeinvitationguarding love
Reflect
  1. “His banner over me was love.” What changes when you know God’s intentions toward you are flown openly, not hidden?
  2. The little foxes spoil the vines. What small, unaddressed thing is quietly eroding a relationship you care about?

Reading frameGuard the small things; they spoil the most.

III
Chapter Three

Seeking by Night & the Royal Procession

“I sought him whom my soul loveth… I found him, and would not let him go.” Song 3:1, 4

A turn into darkness and resolve. The bride rises and searches the city by night until she finds her beloved, holding him fast. The scene then swells into a wedding: Solomon’s carriage rising from the wilderness like pillars of perfumed smoke, crowned on the day of his espousals.

  • The night search through the city streets and the watchmen 3:1–3
  • “Pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense” 3:6
  • The crowned bridegroom — “the day of the gladness of his heart” 3:11
the night searchanticipationthe wedding
Reflect
  1. The bride searches the dark city rather than wait. When has seeking God cost you comfort — and what did you find?
  2. The procession turns private love into public celebration. Where do you keep love hidden that could be honoured openly?

Reading frameWhat you seek in the dark, you hold fast in the light.

IV
Chapter Four

The Bridegroom’s Praise & the Garden Enclosed

“Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee.” Song 4:7

A descriptive love-song head to heart: eyes like doves, a neck like the tower of David. The praise ends in a sealed image — the bride is a garden enclosed, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed, an orchard of pomegranates and spices. Love here is faithful, exclusive, full.

  • “Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse” 4:9
  • “A garden inclosed… a spring shut up, a fountain sealed” 4:12
  • “Awake, O north wind… blow upon my garden” — she opens the garden to him 4:16
faithfulnessbeauty namedthe sealed garden
Reflect
  1. “There is no spot in thee.” How is being seen as wholly lovely different from being told to improve?
  2. The sealed garden is opened by invitation — “blow upon my garden.” What does it mean to give access rather than have it taken?

Reading frameFaithful love is a garden with a gate, not a field without one.

V
Chapter Five

The Lost Beloved & the Search

“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh.” Song 5:2

The shadow side of love. He knocks; she hesitates to rise; when at last she opens, he is gone. She searches the night, is wounded by the watchmen, and pours out her grief in praise — describing her beloved feature by feature to the daughters of Jerusalem.

  • The hesitation, the open door, the absence — love delayed is love nearly lost 5:3–6
  • The watchmen wound the seeking bride 5:7
  • “My beloved is white and ruddy, the chiefest among ten thousand… altogether lovely” 5:10, 16
hesitationlongingdevotion
Reflect
  1. She hesitates to open the door, and he is gone. When has delay or comfort cost you a moment of nearness with God?
  2. Wounded by the watchmen, she still calls him “altogether lovely.” How can loss sharpen love rather than dull it?

Reading frameHesitation is its own kind of answer.

VI
Chapter Six

Restoration & “I Am My Beloved’s”

“I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine: he feedeth among the lilies.” Song 6:3

The chorus asks where he has gone; he is in his garden all along. Reunion brings renewed praise — she is fair as the moon, clear as the sun, terrible as an army with banners. The refrain returns, deeper than before, with possession in the right order: I am his comes first.

  • The refrain matures: “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” 6:3
  • “Fair as the moon, clear as the sun” 6:10
  • The bride named at last — the Shulamite 6:13
reunionmutual loveradiance
Reflect
  1. The refrain now puts “I am my beloved’s” first. Why might belonging come before possessing in healthy love?
  2. He was in his garden the whole time. Where have you assumed God was absent when he was simply where he said he’d be?

Reading frameHe is often nearer than the search suggests.

VII
Chapter Seven

Praise & Mutual Delight

“I am my beloved’s, and his desire is toward me.” Song 7:10

A second song of praise, now from the feet upward, answered by the bride’s own glad desire. She invites him out of the city — to the fields, the villages, the budding vineyards — promising her love where the vines flower. Delight has become a shared journey outward.

  • The refrain’s third form — now centred on his desire toward her 7:10
  • “Let us go forth into the field… let us get up early to the vineyards” 7:11–12
  • “There will I give thee my loves” — love offered in the open country 7:12
delightbelonginggoing out together
Reflect
  1. Now “his desire is toward me.” How does it land to believe God delights in you, not merely tolerates you?
  2. She invites him out to the fields and vineyards. What would it look like to take your faith into ordinary, everyday places?

Reading frameMature love goes out together.

VIII
Chapter Eight

Love Strong as Death

“Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it.” Song 8:7

The song gathers to its great theme. Love asks to be set as a seal — a mark of belonging that cannot be undone — for it is strong as death, fierce as the grave, a flame from the Lord that no flood can drown and no price can buy. The book closes on a call: make haste, my beloved.

  • “Set me as a seal upon thine heart… love is strong as death” 8:6
  • Love unquenchable and unpurchasable — “it would utterly be contemned” 8:7
  • The closing invitation: “Make haste, my beloved” 8:14
the sealunquenchablepermanence
Reflect
  1. Love asks to be a seal — permanent, unremovable. What in your life are you willing to make that kind of commitment to?
  2. “Many waters cannot quench love.” What floods have tested a love of yours, and what kept it from drowning?

Reading frameThe strongest love cannot be bought or quenched.

A Reader’s Glossary

Symbols of the Song

The Garden

The place of intimacy and welcome — an Eden restored where the lovers meet.

The Vineyard

Fruitfulness and care — and the self too: “mine own vineyard have I not kept.”

Spices & Myrrh

Costliness and devotion — the fragrance of a love that holds nothing back.

The Dove

Purity and gentleness — the eyes of the beloved, the voice that signals spring.

The Seal

Belonging made permanent — the mark of a covenant that cannot be unwritten.

Daughters of Jerusalem

The chorus — the watching community that questions, charges, and witnesses the love.

Two Ways to Read

One Song, Two Lenses

The plain sense

Covenant Love

Read first as what it is: a canonical, unembarrassed celebration of married love — desire, faithfulness, and delight held as good gifts. Scripture places the love of bride and bridegroom in its own book and calls it beautiful.

“Many waters cannot quench love.” — 8:7

The long tradition

Christ & the Church

Synagogue and church have also heard a deeper song: God’s love for his people, Christ for his bride. The seeking, the seal, the unquenchable flame echo the Bridegroom of the Gospels and the marriage of the Lamb.

Compare Ephesians 5:25–32 · Revelation 19:7; 21:2
