Chapter Five

The Thirteen
Letters

“All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching.” — 2 Timothy 3:16

❖ ❖ ❖

Thirteen New Testament letters bear Paul’s name — the largest single body of work from any one author in Scripture. They are usually grouped by theme and setting: the great doctrinal letters, the letters written from prison, and the pastoral letters to individual leaders. Below is a full guide to each one. I The Major / Doctrinal Letters

1 · Romans

c. AD 57from Corinthto the church in Rome16 chapters

Paul’s longest and most systematic letter, written to a church he had not yet visited, to introduce himself and lay out the gospel in full. He moves from the universal problem of sin (Jew and Gentile alike), through justification by faith and new life in the Spirit, to God’s faithfulness to Israel and the practical shape of a transformed community.

Key themes: the righteousness of God, justification by faith, sin and grace, life in the Spirit, the place of Israel, living sacrifice and love.

“For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes.” — Romans 1:16

2 · 1 Corinthians

c. AD 55from Ephesusto Corinth16 chapters

Written to a gifted but divided and immature church in a wealthy port city. Paul tackles a string of real problems: factions, lawsuits, sexual immorality, marriage questions, food offered to idols, disorder at the Lord’s Supper, and confusion about spiritual gifts — answering each with the wisdom of the cross.

Key themes: unity in Christ, the cross as God’s wisdom, holiness, the body and its gifts, love (ch. 13), the resurrection of the dead (ch. 15).

“Love is patient and kind… So now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; but the greatest of these is love.” — 1 Corinthians 13:4, 13

3 · 2 Corinthians

c. AD 56from Macedoniato Corinth13 chapters

The most personal and emotional of Paul’s letters. After a painful conflict with the Corinthians and challenges to his authority from rival teachers, Paul defends his ministry — not by boasting in strength, but in his weaknesses and sufferings, through which Christ’s power is shown.

Key themes: comfort in affliction, the new covenant, the ministry of reconciliation, generous giving, strength in weakness, the “thorn in the flesh.”

“My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.” — 2 Corinthians 12:9

4 · Galatians

c. AD 48–55to the churches of Galatia6 chapters

A fiery defense of the gospel of grace. Teachers had told the Galatian believers they must keep the Jewish law — circumcision included — to be true Christians. Paul responds with passion: we are justified by faith in Christ, not by works of the law, and the Spirit, not rule-keeping, produces real righteousness.

Key themes: justification by faith, freedom in Christ, law vs. Spirit, the fruit of the Spirit, sonship and adoption.

“For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery.” — Galatians 5:1

II The Prison Letters

Written, by tradition, during imprisonment — most likely from Rome.

5 · Ephesians

c. AD 60–62from prisonto Ephesus6 chapters

A majestic, unhurried meditation on God’s eternal plan to unite all things in Christ. The first half soars through God’s grace and the creation of one new humanity from Jew and Gentile; the second half works out what that means for daily life, relationships, and spiritual warfare.

Key themes: grace and election, the unity of the church, one new humanity, walking worthily, marriage and household, the armor of God.

“For by grace you have been saved through faith… it is the gift of God, not a result of works.” — Ephesians 2:8–9

6 · Philippians

c. AD 61from prisonto Philippi4 chapters

A warm thank-you letter to Paul’s most supportive church, radiant with joy despite being written from chains. Paul urges humility modeled on Christ’s self-emptying, contentment in every circumstance, and steadfast rejoicing.

Key themes: joy in suffering, the humility of Christ (the great “hymn” of ch. 2), pressing on toward the goal, contentment, partnership in the gospel.

“Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice.” — Philippians 4:4

Read Philippians ↗  ·  Watch overview ↗

7 · Colossians

c. AD 60–62from prisonto Colossae4 chapters

Written to counter a strange mixture of philosophy, mysticism, and rule-keeping threatening the church. Paul’s answer is the towering supremacy of Christ: he is the image of God, the agent of creation, the head of the church, and in him the whole fullness of God dwells — so nothing need be added to him.

Key themes: the supremacy and sufficiency of Christ, freedom from empty tradition, putting on the new self, Christ in you the hope of glory.

“He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation.” — Colossians 1:15

13 · Philemon

c. AD 60–62from prisonto Philemon1 chapter

The shortest of Paul’s letters — a single page — and one of the most tender. Onesimus, a runaway slave, has met Paul and become a believer. Paul sends him back to his master Philemon, not as property but as a beloved brother, gently appealing for grace and even offering to pay any debt himself.

Key themes: forgiveness, reconciliation, Christian brotherhood transcending social status, gospel-shaped relationships.

“Receive him as you would receive me.” — Philemon 17

III The Early Letters

8 · 1 Thessalonians

c. AD 50–51from Corinthto Thessalonica5 chapters

Likely Paul’s earliest surviving letter. Forced to leave a young church abruptly, he writes with affection and relief at good news of their faith, encouraging them to keep living holy lives and reassuring them about believers who have died — for Christ will return.

Key themes: encouragement, holy living, hope in the face of death, the return of Christ, comfort for the grieving.

“Therefore encourage one another with these words.” — 1 Thessalonians 4:18

9 · 2 Thessalonians

c. AD 51–52from Corinthto Thessalonica3 chapters

A follow-up to clear up confusion. Some believed the “day of the Lord” had already come; others had stopped working to wait for it. Paul corrects their understanding of the end, urges steadfastness under persecution, and tells the idle to get back to honest work.

Key themes: standing firm under persecution, clarity about the day of the Lord, the call to keep working faithfully.

“As for you, brothers, do not grow weary in doing good.” — 2 Thessalonians 3:13

IV The Pastoral Letters

Written to individual leaders, on how to shepherd and order the church.

10 · 1 Timothy

c. AD 62–64to Timothy at Ephesus6 chapters

A mentor’s handbook to a younger leader left in charge at Ephesus. Paul advises Timothy on confronting false teaching, on prayer and worship, on the character required of elders and deacons, and on guarding both his life and his doctrine.

Key themes: sound doctrine, qualifications for leadership, godliness, care for the vulnerable, fighting the good fight of faith.

“Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the foremost.” — 1 Timothy 1:15

Read 1 Timothy ↗  ·  Watch overview ↗

11 · 2 Timothy

c. AD 64–67from prison in Rometo Timothy4 chapters

Paul’s last surviving letter — his final words, written from a Roman cell as death approached. With deep warmth he urges Timothy to stay faithful, to guard the gospel, to endure suffering, and to preach the word in season and out. It reads like a torch being passed.

Key themes: faithfulness to the end, guarding sound teaching, endurance, the inspiration of Scripture, finishing the race.

“I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.” — 2 Timothy 4:7

Read 2 Timothy ↗  ·  Watch overview ↗

12 · Titus

c. AD 62–64to Titus on Crete3 chapters

A brief letter to a trusted coworker organizing the young churches on the island of Crete. Paul instructs Titus to appoint sound leaders, to teach healthy doctrine to every group in the church, and to insist that grace produces good works — faith that shows.

Key themes: appointing elders, sound teaching, the link between grace and godliness, doing good.

“The grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us… to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives.” — Titus 2:11–12

A note on authorship. All thirteen are attributed to Paul within the New Testament. Scholars discuss whether some (especially the Pastorals and Ephesians) were written by Paul directly or by close associates in his name — a normal and respectful subject of historical study. The Resources page lists places to explore that conversation. (Hebrews is sometimes linked to Paul but does not name him and is not counted among the thirteen.)
