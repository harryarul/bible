The Pentateuch

From the first word of creation to the edge of the Promised Land — the foundation on which the whole of Scripture is built.

בְּרֵאשִׁית Bereshit שְׁמוֹת Shemot וַיִּקְרָא Vayikra בְּמִדְבַּר Bamidbar דְּבָרִים Devarim

📜 What is the Pentateuch?

“Pentateuch” comes from the Greek for five scrolls. The Jewish people call it the Torah — “instruction” or “law.” It is the opening movement of the Bible and the soil every later book grows out of.

These five books tell one continuous story: God creates a good world, humanity falls, and God begins a rescue plan through one family — Abraham’s — which becomes the nation of Israel. By the final book the people stand on the plains of Moab, the Law given, the covenant renewed, ready to enter the land God promised.

Authorship. Jesus, the New Testament writers, and historic Jewish and Christian tradition attribute the Pentateuch to Moses — he is the central human figure from Exodus onward, and the books themselves describe him writing (Exodus 24:4; Deuteronomy 31:9; Jesus speaks of “the law of Moses” in Luke 24:44). Many modern scholars see later editing and earlier sources woven in, but the books present Moses as the foundational voice.

Dating. By the conservative chronology (built on 1 Kings 6:1), the events run from the patriarchs (Abraham called c. 2091 BC) to the Exodus (c. 1446 BC) and the death of Moses (c. 1406 BC). A widely-held alternative places the Exodus later, c. 1260 BC. Dates before Abraham are not fixed by Scripture; the figures below follow the traditional reckoning and are best read as a framework, not a calendar.

Book One
Genesis
Bereshit · “In the beginning”
AuthorMoses (traditional)
Writtenc. 1446–1406 BC
Events spanCreation → c. 1805 BC
Chapters50

Summary

Genesis is the book of beginnings — of the universe, of humanity, of sin and death, and of God’s plan to undo them. The first eleven chapters move at sweeping speed (creation, the fall, the flood, Babel). Then the lens narrows to one family: God calls Abram and promises to bless all nations through him. The rest follows four generations — Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph — as the promise survives barrenness, famine, betrayal and exile, ending with Israel safe in Egypt.

Outline

  • 1–11 Primeval history: creation, the fall, Cain & Abel, the flood, the tower of Babel
  • 12–25 Abraham: the call, the covenant, Isaac’s birth and near-sacrifice
  • 25–36 Isaac & Jacob: Jacob and Esau, the ladder, wrestling with God, the twelve sons
  • 37–50 Joseph: sold by his brothers, raised in Egypt, saving the family he forgives

Book Two
Exodus
Shemot · “Names”
AuthorMoses (traditional)
Writtenc. 1446–1406 BC
Events spanc. 1876 → 1446 BC
Chapters40

Summary

Centuries after Joseph, his people have multiplied into a nation — and into slavery. God hears their groaning, raises up Moses at the burning bush, and through ten plagues breaks Egypt’s grip. On Passover night Israel is redeemed by the blood of a lamb; at the Red Sea they are delivered; at Sinai they are given the Law and the pattern of the Tabernacle so that the holy God can dwell among them. Exodus is the Old Testament’s great picture of redemption.

Outline

  • 1–4 Slavery & call: Israel oppressed; Moses and the burning bush
  • 5–15 Deliverance: the ten plagues, Passover, the Red Sea
  • 16–18 The journey: manna, water from the rock, Jethro’s counsel
  • 19–24 Covenant at Sinai: the Ten Commandments and the Law
  • 25–40 The Tabernacle: its design, the golden calf, and God’s glory filling it

Book Three
Leviticus
Vayikra · “And He called”
AuthorMoses (traditional)
Writtenc. 1446–1445 BC
Events span~1 month at Sinai
Chapters27

Summary

If Exodus ends with God moving in, Leviticus answers the question: how can a sinful people live next to a holy God? God calls from the Tabernacle and gives the sacrificial system, the priesthood, and laws of clean and unclean — all so that Israel may be holy as he is holy. At its centre stands the Day of Atonement, when the nation’s sin is carried away. It is the most quoted Old Testament book in the New, because it explains why Jesus had to die.

Outline

  • 1–7 The offerings: burnt, grain, peace, sin and guilt sacrifices
  • 8–10 The priesthood: Aaron ordained; the death of Nadab and Abihu
  • 11–15 Clean & unclean: food, childbirth, disease and purity
  • 16 The Day of Atonement: the scapegoat carries sin away
  • 17–27 The Holiness Code: holy living, the feasts, the Jubilee, blessings & curses

Book Four
Numbers
Bamidbar · “In the wilderness”
AuthorMoses (traditional)
Writtenc. 1446–1406 BC
Events span~38–40 years
Chapters36

Summary

Named for its two censuses, Numbers is really the story of a journey that should have taken weeks and took forty years. Standing at the border of the Promised Land, Israel listens to ten fearful spies instead of God — and a whole generation is sentenced to wander and die in the desert. Yet through rebellion, plague and even a pagan prophet’s curses, God keeps his promise alive, raising up a new generation to inherit the land.

Outline

  • 1–10 At Sinai: the first census and order of the camp
  • 11–14 The fatal turn: complaints, the twelve spies, the forty-year sentence
  • 15–19 Wandering & rebellion: Korah’s revolt, Aaron’s budding staff
  • 20–21 The road to Moab: Moses strikes the rock; the bronze serpent
  • 22–36 A new generation: Balaam’s blessings, the second census, preparing for the land

Book Five
Deuteronomy
Devarim · “Words”
AuthorMoses (traditional)
Writtenc. 1406 BC
Events span~1 month, Moab
Chapters34

Summary

The name means “second law” — the Law restated for a new generation on the brink of the land. In a series of farewell sermons, the aged Moses retells the journey, repeats the commandments, and calls Israel to love God with all their heart. He sets before them life and death, blessing and curse, and pleads with them to choose life. Then he blesses the tribes, climbs Mount Nebo, sees the land he may not enter, and dies. It is the book Jesus quotes more than almost any other.

Outline

  • 1–4 First sermon: a look back at the wilderness journey
  • 5–26 Second sermon: the Ten Commandments retold; the Shema; the heart of the Law
  • 27–30 Third sermon: blessings and curses; the call to choose life
  • 31–34 Farewell: Joshua commissioned, the Song of Moses, the death of Moses
